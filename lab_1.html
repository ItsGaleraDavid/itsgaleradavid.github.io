Reclaiming the Sandbox: Notes on Self-Hosting Aethelgard

September 13, 2026

There is a specific kind of exhaustion that comes from modern digital spaces. Everything is pre-packaged, monitored, monetized, and fenced in by corporate templates designed to extract attention rather than foster curiosity. Somewhere along the way, the internet traded its raw, unpolished experimental edge for sterile walled gardens.

I wanted out of that loop. The objective was simple: build a private, persistent virtual world from the ground up, completely untethered from public grids or commercial interests. A return to the hacker ethos of the 2000s—where you owned your stack, understood how the bits moved, and didn't care if things broke because fixing them was the whole point.

The Architecture

The foundation sits on a local instance of OpenSimulator running on port 9000. No cloud dependencies, no external telemetry, just a standalone server process humming away in a local terminal window. The world itself was initialized as Aethelgard—a quiet, isolated region backed by a local SQLite database tracking the terrain heightmaps and object states.

Getting there wasn't entirely frictionless. Modern viewers like Firestorm are heavily optimized for online commercial grids, complete with web-based grid lists designed to herd users toward public spaces. Trying to force it to talk to a local loopback address (http://127.0.0.1:9000/) required bypassing the automated directory tools and feeding the URI directly into the client. Authentication meant dropping into the server console to provision the local root user—Kryvex Root—establishing direct administrative sovereignty over the simulation.

First boot: Kryvex Root standing in the local simulation of Aethelgard.

The Reality of the Terminal

There is an old-school charm in realizing that your entire virtual universe is tethered to a blinking terminal prompt. Close the console window with a careless click, and the simulation evaporates instantly; the physics engine halts, the terrain unloads, and the avatar ceases to exist. That constant reminder of underlying mechanics is precisely the point.

What’s Next

Aethelgard isn't meant to be a polished showcase or a social hub. It’s a laboratory. Now that the pipeline from binary to client is stable, the real work begins: manipulating the physics engine, injecting custom scripts, and treating virtual space not as a finished product, but as raw code waiting to be shaped.
